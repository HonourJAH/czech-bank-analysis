# Findings log: Berka bank

**Q1: Size and growth.** The data covers 4,500 accounts held by 5,369 clients (4,500 owners and 869 authorised users), with 682 loans, 892 cards, 6,471 standing orders and 1,056,320 transactions, about 235 transactions per account. Accounts were opened in waves: 1,139 in 1993 (25.3%), 439 in 1994 (9.8%), 661 in 1995 (14.7%), a peak of 1,363 in 1996 (30.3%) and 898 in 1997 (20.0%). No account was opened after 29 December 1997, so any growth in 1998 comes from activity on existing accounts. No single opening date has more than 13 accounts, so the 1993 figure is not inflated by a bulk entry date.

**Q2: Money flow by year.** Customers deposited more than they withdrew in every year, a net inflow of about 197.2M over 1993 to 1998, peaking at 49.7M in 1996. The share of incoming money that stayed in the bank fell from 8.2% in 1996 to 4.7% in 1997 and 2.8% in 1998, so volume grew but customers withdrew almost as much as they paid in. In 1998 no new accounts were opened, yet transactions rose 13.3% (to 322,277) and money in rose 11.1% (to 960.96M); money in per active account rose from 192,325 to 213,928 and transactions per active account from 63.3 to 71.7, so existing customers used their accounts more. 1993 is a partial year because accounts opened throughout it. Money in includes interest credited and money out includes fees and loan payments, so net flow is not profit.

**Q3: What the transactions are.** Cash dominates: cash deposits and withdrawals are 56.0% of the 1,056,320 transactions and 76.0% of the value moved, and cash withdrawals are the largest single category (434,918, 41.2%), 2.8 times as many as cash deposits. Deposits are large and infrequent (average 15,430) while withdrawals are small and frequent (average 5,379). Card withdrawals are 0.8% of transactions (8,036 across 892 cards). Outgoing transfers outnumber incoming ones 3.2 to 1 but are smaller (average 3,229 against 11,981). Interest credits are 17.3% of transactions but 0.4% of value (27.5M, about 150 each). Among recorded purposes, household payments are the largest debit (498.5M), followed by pensions credited (167.5M), loan payments (55.3M) and insurance (24.2M); statement fees are frequent (155,832) but total only 2.7M. 87.6% of the money moved has no recorded purpose, so purpose figures cover only 12.4% of the value. Interest credited compared with fees is not a profit measure, because loan interest is not in the data.

**Q4: Regions and districts.** North Moravia (793 accounts) and south Moravia (778) hold the most accounts, together 34.9% of the 4,500; Prague, a single district, has 554 (12.3%), and the ten largest districts hold 1,413 accounts (31.4%) out of 77. Penetration is similar across regions, from 0.38 accounts per 1,000 inhabitants in south Moravia to 0.53 in south Bohemia (about 0.44 overall); the data is a sample of the bank's accounts, so this shows how the bank is spread, not its market share. Salaries differ much more than balances: Prague's average salary (12,541) is 45.6% above east Bohemia's (8,611), but its average account balance (37,612) is only 4.5% above (36,008), and balances stay between 36.0K and 37.6K in every region. Some districts outperform their size: Karvina (152 accounts, 0.53 per 1,000) has more accounts than the larger Ostrava (135, 0.42) and Brno (128, 0.33), and Kolin has the highest penetration among the top ten (0.68).

**Q5: The loan portfolio.** The bank lent 103.3M across 682 loans (average 151,410). 76 loans (11.1%) ended in trouble, either finished unpaid (31) or currently in debt (45), but they account for 15.6M, 15.1% of the money, because problem loans are larger: the average problem loan is 205,002 against 144,689 for the others (42% higher), and the 45 loans now in debt average 249,285, more than 2.7 times the 91,641 average of loans that finished paid. Of the 234 loans whose contracts have ended, 13.2% ended unpaid; of the 448 still running, 10.0% are in debt. 403 loans (59.1% of loans, 66.9% of the money) are running without problems and could still fail. Lending grew from 20 loans in 1993 to a peak of 196 in 1997 (30.7M) and fell to 158 in 1998; 51.9% of loans and 53.8% of the money were granted in 1997 and 1998, so about half the portfolio is young and the problem rate may understate where it ends up (tested in Q6). The amounts are those originally granted, not balances owed, and the table has no interest, so this measures credit risk, not profit.

**Q6: Loan outcome by size, term and year.** Loan size is strongly related to trouble: the problem rate is 4.0% for loans under 50,000, 9.4% to 10.1% for the middle bands, and 17.5% for loans of 200,000 to 299,999 and 20.7% for 300,000 and over. The 185 loans of 200,000 or more are 27.1% of loans but 46.1% of problem loans (18.9% fail, against 8.2% for smaller loans, about 2.3 times as often). Duration matters much less: 12-month loans are lowest at 8.4%, and every longer term sits between 11.5% and 12.3% with no steady climb; longer loans are also larger (average 53,636 at 12 months, 244,451 at 60), so the term effect is hard to separate from the size effect. By year granted, the problem rate is stable at 13.3% to 13.9% for 1994 to 1997, while the 158 loans from 1998 show only 2.5% because none has finished yet; excluding 1998, 72 of 524 loans (13.7%) are problem loans, a fairer estimate than the headline 11.1%. The amount is the raw figure with no comparison to the borrower's income, which the data does not contain, so the real driver may be affordability and not size.

**Q7: Loan outcome by client and district.** Each loan was linked to its account's owner and the owner's district. The problem rate varies little by who the borrower is or where they live: by age on the loan date it ranges from 10.4% (30 to 44) to 12.0% (45 to 59); by district salary third, from 10.6% to 11.8%; by district unemployment third, from 10.1% (lowest) to 12.3% (highest); and by gender, 10.5% for men and 11.8% for women, against an overall 11.1%. With only 76 problem loans, gaps of this size are within what chance alone would produce (roughly 2 to 3 percentage points at these group sizes), unlike the loan-size gap in Q6, which is about four times larger than chance. The 60-and-over group has only 17 loans and supports no conclusion. In this data, loan size separates good loans from bad ones and borrower demographics and district conditions do not; lending rules based on age or gender are also restricted in many places, so the result gives no performance reason to use them.

**Q8: Cards.** 892 cards were issued, each on a different account (19.8% of the 4,500 accounts): 659 classic (73.9%), 145 junior (16.3%) and 88 gold (9.9%). Issuing took off late: from 1 card in 1993 to 449 in 1998, which alone is 50.3% of all cards, and 77.5% were issued in 1997 and 1998. Accounts with a card are heavier accounts: they average 249 transactions against 231 (+7.8%), but 1,028,404 of money out against 585,639 (+75.6%) and an average balance of 51,653 against 32,963 (+56.7%), so they transact in larger amounts and not much more often. Card withdrawals are still rare (8,036 in total, about 9 per card), partly because most cards are under two years old. This is an association, not a proven effect of the card: customers with more money may be the ones who take cards, and the activity figures cover the whole life of the account, including before the card existed. The data does not say who holds the junior cards.

**Q9: Account segments.** Accounts were split at the median on activity (transactions per month since opening) and average balance, giving four segments. High activity with high balance is 32.8% of accounts (1,477) but 57.7% of money in, about 1.8 times its share, with an average balance of 50,497 and 6.8 transactions a month. Low activity with low balance is also 32.8% of accounts but only 12.1% of money in (average balance 22,848). Balance separates the segments more than activity does: the two high-balance segments are 50.0% of accounts and 76.4% of money in, while activity only ranges from 4.6 to 6.8 transactions a month. Activity and balance move together (1,477 accounts are high on both and 1,477 low on both, against 773 mixed in each direction). Savers (low activity, high balance, 17.2% of accounts, average balance 47,117) bring in 18.7% of the money, more than their share; high-activity, low-balance accounts (17.2%) bring in 11.5%. The splits at the median are an analytical choice, balance is the average of recorded balances, and the segments describe money flow, not profitability.

**Q10: Overdrafts and loan trouble.** All 76 problem loans are on accounts that were overdrawn at some point and none of the 606 other loans is, a split of exactly 100% and 0%. That is probably not a prediction: a loan flagged "client in debt" very likely coincides with a negative balance, so the status may be defined by the overdraft (this cannot be verified from the data). The overdraft before the loan was granted is the informative test, since it cannot be a consequence of the loan's status: 27 of the 682 loans were granted on accounts that had already been overdrawn, and all 27 became problem loans, against 7.5% (49 of 655) for the rest. That would have flagged 35.5% of problem loans (27 of 76) in advance, with no good loan among the 27, while missing the other 49. 27 loans is a small group, so this is a signal worth testing on more data, not a proven rule.
